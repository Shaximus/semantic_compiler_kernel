## 1. Framing boundary

The stories may be fictional, or mythic, and still dramatize historically real *kinds* of arrangement. That does not establish that the fictional events literally happened, or that the writers encoded a hidden historical record, or that every mythology is the same plot.

The pasted analysis states the boundary this way, and the compiler keeps it: historical recurrence is evidence that humans repeatedly encounter similar structural problems. It is not evidence that every mythology describes the same literal events.

Stargate is a special case only in this limited sense: its creators have said they took existing mythologies and real history and put a science-fiction twist on them. That is a statement about craft, not a warrant for treating the series as a historical source.

## 3. Three questions: Origin, Legitimacy, Capture

The paste isolates three historical questions underneath the stories:

- **Origin.** How does a population come to understand the origin of its civilization, its gods, and its authority?
- **Legitimacy.** What gives someone the right to govern, protect, command, or demand obedience?
- **Capture.** At what point does an institution claiming to represent something greater begin serving its own preservation instead?

These are the questions. The first trio in section 6 answers the legitimacy question in three different ways. The Ori loop in section 4 is the capture question in its tightest fictional form in this source. Origin is recorded and left partly open: creation accounts differ by cult centre and by source layer, and they are not forced into one substrate.

## 4. The Ori worship-power loop as a capture invariant

**Invariant (COMPILER, CANDIDATE, not a class):** an authority whose legitimacy input is produced by its own domination is self-reinforcing capture.

In Stargate, the Ori do not merely enjoy worship. Worship is an input into their power. The more they dominate, the more resources they receive to dominate further. The promise that justifies the worship (ascension under Origin) is, in canon, false.

## 6. First deep dive: the trio and the ten-field matrix

The shared question in the paste is: *How does a cosmic system preserve legitimacy when its own members become sources of instability?*

The three traditions answer it differently. They are not the same story. Some narratives are about succession, some about cosmic maintenance, some about the limits of divine authority. Flattening them into one invariant would manufacture a pattern the sources do not jointly support.

Age of Mythology is used only as a research index for the cast, not as a source for the myths. Original Age of Mythology had Greek, Egyptian and Norse cultures; *The Titans* added the Atlantean/Titan roster; *Retold* ships those four in the base game and adds further pantheons as expansions (section 8).

### 6.1 Greek: the Titan succession (who has the right to rule, and how does the ruling order emerge?)

**EARLIEST SOURCE:** Hesiod, *Theogony*. The well-known three-way division of sky, sea and underworld by lot among Zeus, Poseidon and Hades is not the *Theogony*'s main succession narrative; it is attested in Homer, *Iliad* 15.187–193. Those are different early sources and are not collapsed here.

**LATER RETELLING:** later epic, tragedy, handbook mythography, and Age of Mythology's Greek and Atlantean rosters.

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Primordial origin | *Theogony*: first Chaos, then Gaia, Tartarus and Eros; Gaia produces Uranus. This is a literary cosmogony, not a single "Greek religion" creed. |
| Creation | Gaia and Uranus generate the Titans and other early beings; later the Olympian order is established under Zeus after the Titanomachy. |
| Divine hierarchy | Zeus becomes king of the gods. Poseidon and Hades receive their provinces in the *Iliad* account just cited, not as a *Theogony* quotation. |
| Fragmentation | Uranus confines some of his children; Gaia incites the first revolt. |
| Usurpation | Cronus castrates and overthrows Uranus, then swallows his own children to block a prophesied overthrow. Zeus, hidden by Rhea, later overthrows Cronus. Each new order is founded by seizure. |
| Vessels and intermediaries | Oracles, heroes, and cult. Prometheus, in Hesiod, is an intermediary who acts toward humans (fire and the sacrificial settlement appear across *Theogony* and *Works and Days*). |
| Collective structure | Generations in conflict. The Olympians cooperate against the Titans, then compete among themselves. |
| Destructive cycle | The Titanomachy. A later Gigantomachy is more prominent in later art and retelling than in the *Theogony* itself (flag: do not read it back into Hesiod as if it were the same episode). Zeus also swallows Metis to avert a son who would overthrow him. |
| Restoration | Zeus's order is a *new* settlement, not a restoration of Uranus or Cronus. |
| Unresolved contradiction | If every ruling generation takes power by overthrow, what makes Zeus's rule legitimate rather than merely the latest seizure? The poem presents it as the destined settlement, but the father-overthrown-by-the-son pattern is stopped only by a further pre-emptive act (swallowing Metis), not by a consent procedure. |

**COMPILER reading.** The Greek succession is a poor model for consented key rotation. Mapping it onto `PRINCIPAL_KEY_ROTATE` without that caveat would launder usurpation as a legitimate transfer. The honest analogue is a seized authority that then tries to become the new principal and to block the next seizure.

### 6.2 Egyptian: the Osiris–Horus–Set cycle and Ma'at (what happens when legitimate authority is murdered or displaced, and how is it restored?)

**EARLIEST SOURCE:** the Pyramid Texts (Old Kingdom) allude to Set's attack on Osiris, to a contest or trial, and to Horus's claim; they do not yield one continuous official narrative. The living king is Horus; the deceased predecessor is Osiris.

**LATER RETELLING:** New Kingdom literary *Contendings of Horus and Seth* (Papyrus Chester Beatty I), a sometimes satirical treatment; and, much later, Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* (1st–2nd century CE), a Greek philosophical retelling that supplies the familiar continuous story, including the coffin-trap and the dismemberment sequence. Plutarch is not an Old Kingdom source and is not used here as if he were.

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Primordial origin | Not one account. Heliopolitan tradition starts from Nun and Atum/Ra; Memphite from Ptah; Theban from Amun. These are alternative cult-centre cosmogonies, not a single plot. |
| Creation | A creator god establishes the world and Ma'at (order, truth, rightness) against isfet (chaos, wrong). |
| Divine hierarchy | The living king as Horus, the dead predecessor as Osiris; the king's office is to maintain Ma'at. Ra (and later Amun-Ra) is central in many periods. This is royal theology, not a simple propaganda slogan; see Bickel in section 5. |
| Fragmentation | The killing or displacement of Osiris and the conflict between Horus and Set. |
| Usurpation | Set attacks Osiris and claims the kingship; Horus contests it. The detailed dismemberment-and-reassembly plot is secure as a later retelling (especially Plutarch), not as a reconstruction of the Pyramid Texts. |
| Vessels and intermediaries | The king, the priesthood and temple cult; the dead may be identified with Osiris. These are official channels, not a single "hammer of office." |
| Collective structure | Gods in a legal and familial contest (the *Contendings*). In other contexts Set also defends Ra's barque against Apep. Cooperative and hostile roles coexist. |
| Destructive cycle | Isfet and Apep as recurring threats, especially to the solar journey. This is cyclical maintenance, not a single terminal end like Ragnarök. |
| Restoration | Horus is awarded the living kingship; Osiris rules the dead. A new king's accession reenacts the restoration of rightful authority and the re-establishment of Ma'at. |
| Unresolved contradiction | Set is both murderer/usurper and, in other contexts, a necessary defender against chaos. The tradition does not reduce him to one moral role, and the earliest sources do not give one official continuous narrative. |

**COMPILER reading.** This is the tightest of the three as an analogue of legitimate restoration after usurpation, and therefore of principal release and of a rightful successor taking the office. It is still an analogy: accession rites are not `RELEASE_QUARANTINE`.

### 6.3 Norse: the Æsir–Vanir war and Ragnarök (what happens when the established order cannot prevent its own eventual collapse?)

**EARLIEST SOURCE:** the Poetic Edda, especially *Völuspá* (preserved in the 13th-century Codex Regius, in poems generally treated as older than their manuscript). *Völuspá* alludes to the first war in the world and prophesies Ragnarök, including, in the surviving text, a new earth and Baldr's return.

**LATER RETELLING:** Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda* (*Gylfaginning*, *Skáldskaparmál*) and *Ynglinga saga*, written in Christian-era Iceland in the 13th century. Snorri systematizes, and sometimes euhemerizes, material that the poems leave allusive. Age of Mythology's Norse roster is a further adaptation.

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Primordial origin | Poetic sources are allusive. The systematic Ginnungagap / fire-and-ice opening is presented as a cosmogony in Snorri's *Gylfaginning*. Do not cite Snorri as if he were *Völuspá*. |
| Creation | The gods shape the world from the body of Ymir (present in poetic tradition and in Snorri). |
| Divine hierarchy | Odin as chief of the Æsir. After the war, Vanir figures (Njörðr, Freyr, Freyja) stand inside the combined society. There is no single constitutional charter. |
| Fragmentation | *Völuspá* alludes to the first war (including the Gullveig / Heiðr episode and the Æsir's attempt to destroy her). The fuller hostage-exchange narrative — Njörðr, Freyr and Freyja to the Æsir; Hœnir and Mímir to the Vanir — is given by Snorri (*Ynglinga saga* / *Skáldskaparmál*). |
| Usurpation | This is not the Osiris plot. Loki's role in Baldr's death is betrayal and destabilization more than a seizure of the throne. The giants are hostile and sometimes kin. |
| Vessels and intermediaries | The völva, sacrifice, and Odin's costly acquisition of knowledge (the eye at Mímir's well; the hanging on the tree), attested in the poetic tradition. |
| Collective structure | After the truce, Æsir and Vanir form a combined society that still contains rivalry. Loki stands at the edge. Gods and giants remain opposed. |
| Destructive cycle | Ragnarök is foretold (*Völuspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*): Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Surtr, the deaths of Odin and Thor, the world's burning or sinking. The gods, Odin especially, have knowledge of the end and cannot prevent it. |
| Restoration | *Völuspá* describes a new earth and the return of Baldr (and Höðr). Snorri's *Gylfaginning* 53 narrates survivors who meet at Iðavöllr. UNCERTAIN: whether the restoration ending is a pre-Christian tradition or is shaped by the Christian-era transmission is debated, and is not settled here. |
| Unresolved contradiction | What is the status of an order that can only delay a collapse it already knows is coming? Loki is both companion and destroyer. The aftermath is recorded and still interpretively open. |

**COMPILER reading.** The tight analogue is not "the system will be restored," which is uncertain in the sources. It is the decision to keep a known failure mode visible and to go on maintaining the order without pretending the end has been cancelled.
