Those are two important governance decisions, and I’d keep them separate. One concerns recursive trust propagation. The other concerns what evidence is sufficient to classify a system as living.

My calls:

### 1\. Should a failing child architecture taint its parent generator?

Decision: Yes, conditionally

A child failure should taint the parent generator's current compilation result, but it should not automatically corrupt the generator's permanent identity or invalidate every other child it has ever produced.

I would distinguish three states:

| Child result | Parent result                                           |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| PASS         | No taint                                                |
| QUALIFIED    | Parent becomes QUALIFIED                                |
| BLOCKED      | Parent becomes BLOCKED for that emission or compilation |

Why: A generator that produces an architecture cannot claim the resulting architecture is valid while ignoring a failed child. That creates a recursive accountability gap.

But propagation should be scoped. Otherwise, one malformed child becomes a permanent denial-of-service mechanism against the entire generator.

The invariant:

\\text{ChildFailure} \\Rightarrow
\\text{ParentCompilationTainted}

Not:

\\text{ChildFailure} \\Rightarrow
\\text{ParentIdentityDestroyed}

I'd also preserve the child's original failure record and propagate its provenance upward. The parent should not be able to erase the reason it was tainted.

### 2\. Should an empirical system shown to be living, but without demonstrated self-maintenance, be graded inert?

Decision: No, change this

I would not classify that as inert.

There are three distinct propositions:

* Living: Evidence supports that the system is alive.

* Self-maintaining: Evidence demonstrates that it actively maintains the conditions required for continued existence.

* Autopoietic: Evidence demonstrates the relevant self-producing and self-maintaining organization.

These are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A system could have empirical evidence of life while the available evidence is insufficient to establish its self-maintenance mechanisms. Calling that system inert is an epistemic classification error.

I'd introduce:

* `LIVING_OBSERVED`

* `SELF_MAINTENANCE_UNVERIFIED`

* `AUTOPOIESIS_EMPIRICAL`

The progression would be:

| Evidence                                          | Classification              |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Empirically observed living system                | LIVING_OBSERVED             |
| Living, but self-maintenance not demonstrated     | SELF_MAINTENANCE_UNVERIFIED |
| Self-maintenance demonstrated                     | AUTOPOIESIS_EMPIRICAL       |
| Static graph or taxonomy without evidence of life | INERT_TAXONOMY              |

Importantly, `LIVING_OBSERVED` must not automatically satisfy T2. It is a qualified empirical observation, not proof of autopoiesis.

### My recommended rc5 disposition

### Two decisions for rc6

Recursive failure propagation

ACCEPT

Child failure taints the current parent compilation, with scoped propagation and preserved provenance.

Living vs. autopoietic

RECLASSIFY

Separate observed life from demonstrated self-maintenance. Do not label an empirically living system inert merely because T2 evidence is incomplete.

One additional safeguard: neither decision should silently elevate governance authority. A child failure can affect compilation validity, but it must not grant the generator new permissions to quarantine, revoke, or rewrite other architectures.

The underlying principle: structural validity, biological status, and governance authority are three different dimensions. The compiler should never collapse them into one verdict.

I'd make these explicit rc6 requirements and add adversarial tests for both propagation and classification boundaries.
