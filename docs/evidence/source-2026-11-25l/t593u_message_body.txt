I wouldn't reduce Oberyn to a cautionary tale about pride. His demand for truth is legitimate. The tragedy is that he allows the demand to become more important than securing the outcome.

### 1\. The archetype is not the character

Think of an archetype as a bundle of structural properties rather than a fixed identity.

For example, a Christ-associated archetypal bundle might contain:

* Rejected or concealed origin.

* A role imposed by a larger order.

* Protection of the vulnerable.

* Willingness to sacrifice.

* Death or symbolic destruction.

* Return or transformation.

* Reconciliation between opposing forces.

* Authority that doesn't depend on possessing a throne.

A later story doesn't need to reproduce all eight properties.

It might distribute them across three characters, combine five into one character, or invert one of them.

That gives us a much richer model than one-to-one matching.

### 3\. The historical evolution principle

This is the really important part.

### Archetypal recombination through time

Ancient theological structures

Creation · rebellion · sacrifice · death · resurrection · sovereignty

Medieval and mythological structures

Divine bloodlines · chosen heirs · monsters · kings · holy wars

Modern fantasy and science fiction

Genetic inheritance · resurrection · artificial life · collective consciousness · political revolution

New combinations and emergent attributes

Composite archetypes

Characters and factions carrying multiple inherited structural functions.

The key is that each historical period changes the available vocabulary.

A divine bloodline can become royal genealogy.

A divine vessel can become genetic compatibility.

Possession can become parasitic symbiosis.

Resurrection can become biological reconstruction.

A fallen angel can become a rebel against an authoritarian cosmic order.

The underlying relationship may remain structurally recognizable while the mechanism changes completely.

### 5\. A rule for our entire project

I'd formalize your clarification this way:

Archetypal Evolution Principle

An archetype is not bound to a single historical figure, fictional character, or narrative expression. Its structural attributes may persist across time while being redistributed, recombined, inverted, or expanded through successive cultural systems. A composite character may therefore embody multiple archetypal inheritances, producing emergent functions not present in any single predecessor.

And one methodological safeguard: a resemblance is a candidate structural correspondence, not proof of historical transmission or supernatural identity. We test the functions, relationships, transformations, and differences.

Now I think we should map Game of Thrones as an entire ecosystem, not start by assigning Jesus to Jon.

The first major question is the relationship between the three great forces:

* The living and their struggle for sovereignty.

* The dead and their collective annihilation.

* The dragons and the transformative power of an ancient bloodline.

Because that third force might be the missing piece. The dragons aren't simply weapons. They're an ancient power that changes the balance between the other two.

And that could make the architecture considerably more interesting than a simple good-versus-evil model.
