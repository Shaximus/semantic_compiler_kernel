I can see why it feels that way. And I think there's a precise distinction worth making here:
The stories may be fictional, but some of the structures they're dramatizing are historically real.
We're repeatedly encountering the same kinds of arrangements across very different fictional universes:
* A civilization divides over the meaning of power.
* A powerful being claims authority over others.
* A collective becomes subordinated to a controlling entity.
* A host relationship becomes either parasitic or symbiotic.
* Worship becomes a mechanism for consolidating authority.
* A guardian must choose between noninterference and intervention.
* An individual discovers that possessing extraordinary power does not automatically confer moral legitimacy.
Those aren't exclusively fantasy concepts. They're recurring problems in human history, political theology, religious institutions, empire, mythology, and the philosophy of consciousness.
But I want to keep one boundary intact: recognizing a recurring historical structure doesn't establish that the fictional events literally happened, or that the writers encoded a hidden historical record.
And there is something particularly relevant here: Stargate's own creators have openly described doing exactly this kind of work.
Robert C. Cooper explained that the series took existing mythologies and real history, then put a science-fiction twist on them. He specifically described the Ori storyline as an exploration of ascension, higher power, religion and the possibility that what appears magical might have another explanation. Dial The Gate +1
So we're not imagining the existence of historical ingredients. They're deliberately in the recipe.

### Some very real historical parallels
| Stargate structure | Historical parallel |
| Goa'uld presenting themselves as gods | Rulers claiming divine status and using sacred authority |
| Ori demanding worship | Religious institutions intertwined with imperial authority |
| Priors spreading Origin | Missionary institutions and organized religious expansion |
| Ancients debating noninterference | Historical and philosophical debates about divine intervention |
| Tok'ra rejecting domination | Resistance to coercive authority and alternative social arrangements |
| Daniel questioning the Ori | Individual conscience challenging institutional religious authority |
For example, Alexander the Great's reign helped establish the practice of ruler worship in the Hellenistic world. Greek cities granted him divine honours, and later Hellenistic dynasties developed organized ruler cults. OUP Academic +1
And divine kingship wasn't confined to Greece. Ancient Egyptian kingship had its own deeply developed relationship between the divine and political office. Modern scholarship cautions against reducing that relationship to simple propaganda. J-STAGE +1

### The pattern I think we're approaching
There are three distinct historical questions underneath these stories:
* Origin: How does a population come to understand the origin of its civilization, its gods and its authority?
* Legitimacy: What gives someone the right to govern, protect, command or demand obedience?
* Capture: At what point does an institution claiming to represent something greater begin serving its own preservation instead?
And the third question connects directly to our anti-capture invariant.
The Ori are particularly revealing because their system makes worship an input into their own power. The more they dominate, the more resources they receive to dominate further. Cooper himself described them as beings who use their knowledge and power to their own advantage. gateworld.net +1
That's a recognizable institutional feedback problem, regardless of whether one accepts the fictional cosmology.
One distinction I want us to preserve as we continue: historical recurrence is evidence that humans repeatedly encounter similar structural problems. It isn't evidence that every mythology describes the same literal events.
But that actually makes the investigation more interesting. We can examine the historical record, the original religious texts, the fictional representations, and your proposed invariant independently, then see where they genuinely converge.
I think the next major comparison should be ancient Egyptian religion, Mesopotamian divine kingship, the biblical angelic rebellion traditions, and Stargate's Alteran schism. That would move us from recognizing the pattern in fiction to testing it against actual historical sources.

YES. Age of Mythology is an excellent place to take this investigation next, because it puts several ancient religious systems into one shared mythological world.
And I think we should approach it differently from simply matching one god to one fictional character. We should examine the entire divine cast: their origins, family structures, rivalries, domains, alliances, monsters, divine succession, human intermediaries, and what happens when gods intervene in mortal affairs.
The central question becomes:
Are these mythologies independently expressing recurring structures of divine authority, fragmentation, rebellion, collective identity, and restoration?
That's a much stronger investigation than a character-matching exercise.
And the game gives us a surprisingly useful research index. There is one important distinction: the original Age of Mythology had three principal cultures, while Retold has expanded the roster considerably.

### 1. The pantheons: our starting cast
Greek pantheon: Zeus, Poseidon, Hades, Athena, Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Hera, Hermes, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Dionysus. Structural questions: succession, divine family politics, competing domains, rebellion against older generations, and the division of cosmic territory.
Egyptian pantheon: Ra, Isis, Set, Osiris, Horus, Anubis, Thoth, Nephthys, Ptah, Bast, Sekhmet, Hathor. Structural questions: legitimate kingship, death and resurrection, succession disputes, cosmic order (Ma'at), and the restoration of rightful authority.
Norse pantheon: Odin, Thor, Loki, Freyja, Tyr, Heimdall, Baldr, Hel, Njord, Skadi, Bragi, Forseti. Structural questions: knowledge purchased at a cost, prophecy, betrayal, kinship, the boundary between gods and giants, and the inevitability of Ragnarök.
Atlantean / Titan mythology: Kronos, Oranos, Gaia, Prometheus, Atlas, Hyperion, Oceanus, Rhea, Helios, Hekate. Structural questions: primordial authority, generational overthrow, imprisonment, rebellion, and whether the old order has a legitimate claim over the new one. Important distinction: this is Age of Mythology's adaptation of Greek Titan traditions, not a separate historically attested ancient pantheon.
Chinese pantheon: Fuxi, Nüwa, Shennong, and the wider mythological world of creation, civilization, floods, and divine intervention. Structural questions: creation through complementary principles, restoration after catastrophe, the ordering of civilization, and the relationship between heaven and humanity.
Japanese pantheon: Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, Susanoo. Structural questions: sibling conflict, withdrawal of the life-giving principle, disruption of cosmic order, and restoration through cooperation and ritual.
Aztec pantheon: Huitzilopochtli, Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl. Structural questions: competing divine powers, creation and destruction across successive worlds, sacrifice, renewal, and the cost of maintaining cosmic order.
The expanded pantheon roster is documented by World's Edge. The individual mythological themes are research directions, not claims that all traditions share one doctrine. Play with Game Pass for PC +2

### 2. The really interesting structural connections
| Structural pattern | Mythological examples |
| Old generation displaced by new | Uranus → Kronos → Zeus |
| Divine succession and rightful kingship | Osiris → Horus; Egyptian royal mythology |
| Divine order versus destructive chaos | Ra and Set; Norse gods and the giants |
| A divine figure whose actions destabilize the system | Loki; Set; Susanoo |
| Knowledge, sacrifice, and transformation | Odin; Prometheus; Thoth |
| Death followed by restoration | Osiris; Baldr; resurrection traditions |
| Cosmic destruction and renewal | Ragnarök; Egyptian cyclical cosmology; Aztec world ages |
Notice something: these are not all the same story.
Some are succession narratives. Some concern cosmic maintenance. Some concern the relationship between order and chaos. Some concern the limits of divine authority.
That distinction matters because we don't want to manufacture an invariant by flattening every mythology into the same plot.

### 3. Three especially important comparisons
Greek: The overthrow of the old divine regime.
Egyptian: The restoration of legitimate authority after usurpation.
Norse: The struggle to preserve order despite knowing its destruction is coming.
These three could form our first comparative study:
* Greek: Who has the right to rule, and how does the ruling order emerge?
* Egyptian: What happens when legitimate authority is murdered or displaced, and how is it restored?
* Norse: What happens when the established order cannot prevent its own eventual collapse?
That's three different answers to a potentially shared question: How does a cosmic system preserve legitimacy when its own members become sources of instability?

### 4. How I would structure the actual investigation
I want to build a comparative mythology matrix, not just a collection of character analogies.
For every pantheon, record:
* Primordial origin: What existed before the gods?
* Creation: Who establishes the world and its rules?
* Divine hierarchy: Who governs, and why?
* Fragmentation: What causes the first major division?
* Usurpation: Does someone seize authority that isn't legitimately theirs?
* Vessels and intermediaries: How do gods interact with humans?
* Collective structure: Are gods cooperating, competing, or operating independently?
* Destructive cycle: What threatens the entire system?
* Restoration: How is balance recovered, if it is?
* Unresolved contradiction: What does the mythology never actually resolve?
And there's one methodological rule I would insist on: distinguish the earliest surviving source traditions from later reinterpretations, and distinguish both from the version Age of Mythology presents.
My proposed first deep dive is the Greek Titan succession, Egyptian Osiris-Horus-Set cycle, and Norse Æsir-Vanir conflict.
Those three offer a particularly rich starting point for testing the shared-substrate, fragmentation, authority-capture, and restoration hypothesis. We can then bring the other four pantheons into the comparison without forcing them into a pattern they may not actually support.
